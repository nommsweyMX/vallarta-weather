Hurricane Simon and Puerto Vallarta: what the models said, what happened, and how much to trust the next 48 hours

A verification of 25 forecast models (physics, AI, ensembles and the National Hurricane Center) against the observed storm, with the uncertainty shown rather than hidden, and a point-by-point reply to the uploaded analysis Comprehensive Verification and Theoretical Analysis of Dynamical and AI-Driven Tropical Cyclone Forecasting.

Based on NHC advisory 18 (3 pm CST) · SMN aviso 34 (12:00 CST) · ATCF decks · ECMWF open data · NOAA open data (GFS, GEFS, AIGFS, NBM, HAFS) · NOAA/CIRA AI-model archive · METAR MMPR · GOES-18
Not an official forecast. Follow the SMN, the NHC and Protección Civil. Emergencies: 911. Everything here is an independent reading of public data; it is meant to help you interpret the official products, not replace them.

1. The short version

  • Where Simon is. At 3 pm Sunday the center was at 19.6N 105.8W, 90 km south of Cabo Corrientes, with 130 mph (115 kt) winds, moving NNW at 9 km/h, roughly parallel to the coast. The eye is brushing the Jalisco coast but is not forecast to cross it in the next two days.
  • Which models were right. Over the last 48 hours the best position forecasts came from Google DeepMind's cyclone model (mean error 33 km; 5 km off at 3 pm today), the NHC's corrected consensus (38 km) and the NHC official forecast (47 km). The least accurate of the fully verified models were the US regional hurricane models (HAFS-B, COAMPS-TC, HAFS-A, HMON) and UKMET; the GFS-driven AI runs (Aurora, FourCastNet, Pangu) did as badly or worse at the leads they could be checked. From Saturday evening HAFS-A, HAFS-B and UKMET carried Simon about 150 km too far north, into or past Banderas Bay, and HMON and COAMPS-TC had done the same (140–280 km) in their Saturday daytime runs. That is where the hurricane-wind scenario for Vallarta came from.
  • Intensity. Every gridded AI model collapsed Simon to a tropical storm within 12–24 hours while it stayed a category 4; so did the physics ensembles on their 25-km public grids. The best intensity guidance for this storm was the NHC itself, its corrected consensus, the statistical SHIPS model and DeepMind's cyclone model.
  • Vallarta so far. The airport's reports show a peak wind of 17 km/h (9 kt) in the last 48 hours, with a 19-hour gap in reports from Saturday 6 pm to Sunday 1 pm, and about 9 mm (0.35 in) of rain from one shower in the six hours to 1:44 pm. Residents in the city, including the person this report is for, saw no rain through early afternoon; there is no official gauge in town. Of the seven gridded models that give a rain total, five gave Vallarta more rain than fell; ECMWF AIFS (7 mm) and AIGFS (10 mm) matched. Almost all gridded models gave less wind than measured; the track-based estimates gave more.
  • What to expect. The weighted consensus puts the closest pass about 100–110 km west-southwest of the city around 11:30 pm tonight, with the center still a hurricane. For the city: gusty wind from the east and east-southeast, sustained roughly 30–45 km/h with gusts 45–60 km/h on high floors, from Sunday afternoon to about 4 am Monday, peaking around 7–8 pm and staying near that level until the center passes around midnight; little rain tonight; a chance of moderate rain Monday afternoon when the wind turns onshore; a dangerous sea on the outer coast (SMN: waves of 8 to 10 m and a surge of 1.5 to 2.5 m on the Jalisco coast).
  • The uncertainty, in one line. The NHC puts Vallarta's chance of tropical-storm-force wind at 8% and of hurricane-force wind under 1%; of the models that have earned trust in this storm, none brings the center within 90 km of the city. A 30–40 km shift east of the line, inside the two-day average error of most models and at the edge of the best three's (33–47 km), would bring tropical-storm-force gusts over the bay tonight, with sustained winds just short of it.
  • On the uploaded analysis. Its physics and its description of the AI intensity problem hold up. Its model-verification table does not: the numbers have no source, and several contradict the official record (HAFS-B was the worst track model of the last two days, not a 51-nm one; no dynamical model showed "absolute supremacy" on intensity). It also omits the one model that actually won, and the 15 inches of rain it uses as an example for Vallarta is a hypothetical, not a forecast: the city has had under 10 mm so far.

2. Where Simon is and what is forecast

NHC advisory 18 (3 pm CST): center 19.6N 105.8W, 115 kt, 947 mb, moving 335° at 5 kt. Forecast: 20.2N 106.1W and 90 kt by midnight tonight (near the coast), 21.0N 106.6W and 80 kt by noon Monday, 21.6N 106.9W and 75 kt by midnight Monday, then a bend back toward the Nayarit–Sinaloa coast by Wednesday, still a 75-kt hurricane in the NHC forecast (a Hurricane Watch is in effect from north of El Roblito to Bahía Tempehuaya), weakening inland on Thursday. The discussion calls the track forecast "low confidence" and notes the guidance has shifted west and slower again. The wind-speed probabilities for Puerto Vallarta are 8% for tropical-storm force and under 1% for hurricane force.

SMN aviso 34 (12:00 CST) keeps extraordinary point rainfall (more than 250 mm) for coastal and western Jalisco, 150–250 mm in Colima, 75–150 mm in southern Nayarit, waves of 8 to 10 m on the Jalisco coast, a storm surge of 1.5 to 2.5 m on the Jalisco and Colima coasts, and gusts of 240–250 km/h on the Jalisco coast near the center, decreasing through the day. The SMN's wording is "Jalisco (coast and west)", which on paper includes the city; the NHC's "isolated maximum storm totals of 30 inches" are for "portions of the states of Michoacán, Colima and Jalisco". My reading, not the agencies': with the center to the southwest, the heaviest totals fall on the windward sierra south and west of the bay, while the city sits on the lee side in descending easterly flow, as today's dry afternoon showed. That reading changes once the wind turns onshore.

3. The uploaded analysis: what it argues, and where the data agrees

The document is a long essay in five parts: the physics of tropical cyclones and of this coastline; the architecture of AI weather models and why they under-forecast intensity (training on 31-km reanalysis, a mean-squared-error loss that rewards smoothing, the "double penalty"); pathways to fix that (high-resolution training, physics-informed networks, neural ODEs, and ECMWF's AIFS-TC post-processing); a model-verification table for Simon; and a critique of the NHC cone of uncertainty as a communication device. Much of it is sound. The parts that matter for someone deciding what to do tonight are the verification table and the rainfall claim, and those are the weakest parts. 4. The verification, model by model

Every model was scored the same way. Position: the error of each forecast made in the 48 hours before 3 pm Sunday, measured against the NHC's official positions every six hours and against the advisory-18 position; the ranking score is the mean at 12, 24 and 36 h lead. Vallarta wind and rain: what the run of Saturday 6 pm gave the city up to 3 pm Sunday, against the airport record (max 9 kt in the available reports; about 9 mm of rain by 1:44 pm). Gridded models were sampled at their nearest grid point to the city; track models give no point wind, so their wind is the value implied by the NHC wind structure, or the model's own wind radii where the deck carries them, placed on the model's line.

What stands out

  • The best three were not the newest or the highest-resolution models. DeepMind's cyclone model, the NHC's corrected consensus and the NHC official forecast. The simple climatology-and-persistence baseline (53 km) beat GFS, GEM, UKMET and all five US regional hurricane models (HWRF, HMON, HAFS-A, HAFS-B, COAMPS-TC) over this window, which is unusual and says the storm's motion was steady while many models were looking for a turn.
  • The regional hurricane models failed in the direction that mattered. HAFS-A, HAFS-B, HMON and COAMPS-TC were 150–280 km too far north at various cycles, bringing a category-4 core into Banderas Bay. Because these are the models the NHC leans on for structure, that is also why the official forecast was 60–140 km too far east and north on Friday and Saturday, and why the NHC's own probability of hurricane-force wind in Vallarta reached 15–20% late Friday and early Saturday (section 6).
  • Among gridded AI models, ECMWF AIFS and its ensemble did well on position (50 and 58 km; 43 of 51 ensemble members within 100 km of the true center at 3 pm). The GFS-initialised models in the NOAA/CIRA archive did not: FourCastNet and Aurora were 160–200 km off at 3 pm, and the NOAA AIGFS dissipated the storm inland and ended 210 km too far north.
  • Local wind and rain are beyond all of them. Nine gridded models put the city's peak wind at 4–12 km/h against 17 km/h measured, and five of the seven that carry rain gave more than fell. The bay, the city and the sierra do not exist on a 25–50 km grid; the NBM, the US blend that corrects for terrain, reaches Vallarta only through its oceanic domain (wind only); its 36 km/h was above the airport's 17 km/h, as were the track-based estimates (28–75 km/h), while the coarse grids were below it.

5. The intensity problem, shown on Simon

Three different failures are visible in one picture. The gridded AI models (right, pink band and the AIFS line) start from the same 93-kt GFS or 67-kt AIFS analysis and lose half their wind in the first six hours, then settle at 15–35 kt: the smoothing the document describes, plus a 0.25° grid that cannot hold a 20-km eyewall. The regional hurricane models (left, HAFS-A and HAFS-B) start at the right intensity and crash to about 35 kt by 18–24 h, not because of smoothing but because they ran the storm into the Sierra Madre 150 km north of where it went. The global physics models on their 25-km public grids (ECMWF IFS, GFS) sit in between: IFS kept 65–88 kt, GFS fell to 19 kt as it too took the center inland.

The guidance that worked was the guidance that treats intensity as its own problem: the NHC forecaster (135 kt at 12 h, 100 kt at 24 h), the corrected consensus and the DeepMind cyclone model (104 kt at 12 h, 91 kt at 24 h), which is trained on cyclone tracks and intensities rather than on gridded fields. That supports one half of the document's prescription, a cyclone-specific correction on top of the global model (what AIFS-TC and the DeepMind model are), and does not test the other half, physics-informed networks.

Two caveats. The intensity verification covers one storm and a handful of cycles. And "right" here means right for Vallarta's purposes, where the question was whether a category-4 core would reach the bay; for the coast between Playa Pérula and Cabo Corrientes, which the eastern eyewall is brushing this evening, the intensity the NHC carried was the relevant number, and it was right.

6. Uncertainty, shown rather than hidden

The document's criticism of the cone is that it looks certain. The honest alternative is to show how the forecast itself moved, how wide the ensembles are, and what the models' own error record implies for tonight.

From Friday afternoon to Saturday night (advisories 10 to 15) the chance of hurricane-force wind in the city moved between 4% and 20%, and the chance of tropical-storm-force wind between 60% and 76%. That is the period in which the regional hurricane models carried the core into the bay. Since then it has gone 75%, 69%, 56% and, at 3 pm today, 8%: almost the whole drop came in one advisory, between 9 am and 3 pm, as the storm held its north-northwest heading and the guidance shifted west. A number that can fall 48 points in six hours can also climb back. Anyone who read the probabilities rather than the cone saw a "likely tropical-storm wind, unlikely hurricane" picture on Saturday and a "probably neither" picture today. The cone, drawn around the official line that was 60–140 km too far east, looked the same both days.

What could still go wrong

  • An eastward wobble. The center is 90 km south of Cabo Corrientes. The NHC's 34-kt wind radius in the northeast quadrant is about 93 km. A 30–40 km shift east of the forecast line, inside the two-day average error of most models and at the edge of the best three's (33–47 km), would bring tropical-storm-force gusts over the bay tonight, with sustained winds just short of it. Treat the 8% as "unlikely", not "impossible".
  • Rain when the wind turns. While the center is southwest of the city the flow descends the sierra and the city stays dry, as it did today. Once the center passes to the northwest (the consensus says Monday afternoon) the wind turns onshore and showers become likely; the gridded models give 3–24 mm for the next 24 h, GraphCast 106 mm, but GraphCast has been the wettest miss of all. The SMN's extraordinary totals apply to the coast and sierra south of the bay, where the Cuale and Pitillal rivers rise; river levels, not local rain, are what to watch in the lower town.
  • The sea. This is the one hazard every source agrees on: waves of 8 to 10 m on the outer coast, a surge of 1.5 to 2.5 m, water pushed into the bay mouth, dangerous conditions on the malecón and the southern beaches through Monday.
  • Our own estimates. The city wind numbers are the NHC wind field on the NHC line, capped by the wind value the NHC odds give a 50% chance of, and scaled by building exposure; they are not a measurement and they move with every advisory. The rain field is a satellite estimate carried along the NHC line, and there is no radar in the region. The verification itself rests on one to three forecasts per model and lead.

7. Risk communication: my own input

Where I agree with the document. The cone communicates the wrong thing to a resident. It answers "where might the center be" with a line and a two-thirds envelope, and people read it as the storm's size and as a yes-or-no about their town. For Vallarta the right question was never where the center would be; it was "what wind reaches my building, when, and what rain reaches the rivers above me". The NHC does publish the answer to the first part, as wind-speed probabilities and as earliest-arrival times, and this weekend those numbers told a calmer and more accurate story than the cone did. The 2026 change that draws inland watches and warnings inside the cone helps, but as long as a single black line is on the graphic, people will anchor on it.

Where I would push back. The document treats AI versus physics as the main axis. On this storm the useful axis was "models that treat the cyclone as its own object" (the NHC forecaster, the consensus aids, DeepMind's cyclone model, and in the document's own account AIFS-TC) versus "models that treat it as a feature on a global grid" (every gridded AI model and the 25-km ensembles). And the table at the center of its verification section is not a verification; it is a set of plausible-looking numbers without a source. In a document meant to be forwarded during a hurricane, that matters more than the quality of the physics chapters.

What we did instead, and its limits. The six numbered graphics on the shared page (Figure 3 and A1–A5 here) and the one-page building summary (Figure 1) replace the cone with: the NHC's probabilities turned into a wind number per building and floor; a timeline of when it starts, peaks and ends; a map of where water and slopes are the danger; and an explicit scorecard of which models to believe, updated each advisory. The limits are real: no radar, 25–50 km model grids that cannot see the bay, wind estimates that lean on the NHC's probability product, and a verification sample of two days. The scorecard is also only as good as the next advisory; it is rebuilt at each one.

Three recommendations for the next storm. Lead with the wind-speed probabilities and the earliest-arrival time for named places, not the cone. Show the ensemble members as dots, as in Figure 3, so the spread is visible. And publish a running scorecard of model performance for the storm in progress; it would have taken the hurricane-model scenario off the table a day earlier this time.

Appendix B. Methods and sources

Position verification

Forecasts from every cycle between 3 pm Friday and noon Sunday (09/21Z to 11/18Z), verified up to 3 pm Sunday (11/21Z), were interpolated to the NHC best-track times (6-hourly) and to the advisory-18 position (11/21Z, 19.6N 105.8W). Errors are great-circle distances. The score is the mean error at 12, 24 and 36 h lead. Track-type models come from the public ATCF a-deck (late versions: AVNO, CMC, UKX, NVGM, HWRF, HMON, HFSA, HFSB, CTCX, GDMN, CEMN, AEMN, HCCA, TVCN, CLP5, OFCL); gridded models were tracked as the minimum sea-level pressure inside 15–24N, 110–101W, with points on the box edge discarded. ECMWF IFS, AIFS and the two ECMWF ensembles come from the open-data tropical-cyclone track files (BUFR) and GRIB fields; GFS, GEFS mean, AIGFS, NBM and HAFS-A from the NOAA open-data buckets; FourCastNet v2, Pangu-Weather, GraphCast and Aurora from the NOAA/CIRA archive of AI models run from GFS analyses (no 6 pm Friday run exists there, which is why their scores lack a 36-h lead).

Intensity

Track models: the a-deck maximum wind. Gridded models: the strongest 10-m wind within 2° of the model's own low. Whole-storm intensity errors are the mean absolute error at 12–36 h over all cycles of the storm.

Vallarta wind and rain

Observations: METAR MMPR (airport) for the 48 h to 3 pm Sunday; there are no reports between 6 pm Saturday and 1 pm Sunday, and no official gauge in the city. Gridded models: bilinear interpolation of the 10-m wind to 20.61N 105.23W; rain accumulated from the run start at the nearest grid point, with the maximum of the four surrounding points also kept. Track models: a parametric wind field with the NHC wind radii of the same cycle, or the model's own radii where the deck carries them, evaluated at the city over open water, before any land or building exposure factor.

The resident graphics

City wind: the parametric wind field on the NHC line evaluated at the city, capped by the wind value at which the NHC wind-speed probabilities give 50%, then scaled by exposure factors for each building (0.90 for an exposed high floor, 0.78 for a sheltered mid floor, 0.70 for a street-level house). Bay-wide fields: a parametric wind model on the NHC line with the official radii; rain from the GOES-18 rainfall-rate product carried in a storm-relative frame along the NHC line, calibrated only downward, never up. Flooding and slope layers: Puerto Vallarta risk atlas (2024), CENAPRED, terrain tiles. All times are Vallarta time (UTC−6).
