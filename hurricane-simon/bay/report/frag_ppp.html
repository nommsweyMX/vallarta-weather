10. Training, collaboration and a public-private partnership: what this coast needs

This section steps back from Simon, and it is my own reading of what is needed and how a realistic partnership could pay for it; where a number is my estimate rather than a sourced figure, it says so. The storm passed Puerto Vallarta by. For four days the deterministic line on every product a resident was likely to see, including ours, pointed the center at the bay; the probabilities and the discussions, which few residents read, did not. The global models were not the weak link. The weak link was everything between a 28-km global grid and a window in Amapas, and that gap is local, fixable, and will matter more as the storms on this coast change.

10.2 Not a one-storm problem

Simon is the latest in a list of eight hurricanes since 2002, drawn in Figure 10 from the NHC's own reports, that strengthened quickly within a day or two of Mexico's Pacific coast; it is not an exhaustive list. Kenna (2002) went from 40 to 140 kt in 42 hours and hit San Blas; Patricia (2015) gained 105 kt in 24 hours on its way to the Jalisco coast; Lidia (2023) gained 45 kt in 18 hours and made landfall 35 nautical miles south-west of Puerto Vallarta as a category 4; Otis (2023) gained 90 kt in 21 hours and destroyed Acapulco; John (2024) went from a 35-kt tropical storm to a category 3 in 24 hours and was not forecast to be a hurricane in any of the three advisories issued in the 30 hours before landfall. The mean official intensity errors over those storms ran from 6 kt (Lidia) to 35 kt (Patricia) at 24 hours and from 7 to 58 kt at 48 hours; in Kenna, Patricia and Otis they were 27–35 kt and 52–58 kt, between one and three Saffir-Simpson categories, and the NHC wrote after Patricia and again after Otis that nothing in its guidance had anticipated the strengthening. Simon, like Roslyn and Erick, had its rapid intensification forecast in advance (the advisory on Thursday night called for 100 kt in 24 hours and got 105), and even then the 48-hour value was 25 kt low.

What the climate adds. The IPCC's sixth assessment rates it likely that the global share of category 3–5 storms and the frequency of rapid intensification have increased over the last 40 years, and very likely that peak winds and the share of category 4–5 storms will rise with warming; its central estimates are a 10–13% increase in the category 4–5 share and an 11–14% increase in storm rain rates at 1.5–2 °C. Two findings bear directly on this coast. Balaguru and colleagues (2024) find that intensification rates close to coasts have risen by about 3 kt per 24 hours since 1979 and are projected to keep rising as wind shear near coastlines weakens. Radfar and colleagues (2026) find the clearest upward trend in storms that rapidly intensify during marine heatwaves in the eastern Pacific and the North Atlantic, and name Otis as the example. The Pacific tide gauges give a 25-year storm-driven water-level excess of about 0.85–0.95 m today, on top of a sea level that is extremely likely to keep rising. Mexico's president said in June, as reported by Zeta Tijuana, that the Pacific is the least-observed part of the country's weather network. None of this says the next storm will hit Vallarta. It says the next storm that threatens it will more often strengthen late, close in, where today there is nothing to measure it with.

10.3 What the models can and cannot do, and what "AI plus physics" should mean

The verification in this report (sections 6 and 7) matches what the NHC found over a full season. In 2025, the first year the NHC used AI models in operations, Google DeepMind's cyclone ensemble beat the official forecast and every consensus aid on track at 48–120 hours in the eastern Pacific, and it was the only model to beat the official track at nearly every lead time for Priscilla, the storm that ran up this coast a year ago. On intensity the same model was best only out to 48 hours; beyond 60 hours the physics-based regional models (HAFS) were still the best guidance. The gridded AI models, trained on reanalysis that smooths hurricane cores, under-forecast intensity badly: the first NHC-style evaluation (DeMaria and colleagues, 2024) found their intensity errors worse than climatology, and ECMWF's own analysis of its AI model over 70 storms in 2025 found a mean bias of −28.8 kt, with errors of 71 kt in rapid-intensification cases. Simon reproduced all of this: DeepMind's cyclone model had the best positions (33 km mean error) and sensible intensities (104 kt when the storm was 130 kt); every gridded AI model collapsed Simon to a tropical storm; the regional physics models carried the center 100–170 km too far north and, taking it toward land, lost the intensity as well.

The most instructive result of the year is how ECMWF fixed its intensity problem. A post-processor trained on nine seasons of past forecasts and observations (2016–2024), using gradient-boosted trees and a small convolutional network on the storm-centred fields, cut the AI model's intensity error from 28.9 to 10.8 kt, level with DeepMind's model and with the NHC's official forecast; the authors note the system was built in hours. The lesson is not that AI is magic. It is that the cheapest, fastest-improving layer in the whole chain is a statistical correction trained on data, and that the layer is only as good as the observations it is trained on. For track and intensity those observations exist (best tracks, aircraft, satellite). For wind and rain in Puerto Vallarta they do not: there is no archive of what the bay actually does when a hurricane passes 100 km offshore, because nothing has been measuring it.

So "AI plus physics" for a coast like this should mean four specific things:

  • Physics for structure and intensity, AI ensembles for track and probability. Use the regional hurricane models for the wind structure and for intensity beyond about 48–60 hours, where the 2025 verification shows they still lead, weighted by the running scorecard; use the AI cyclone ensembles (50–64 members, minutes to run) for where the center goes and how uncertain that is; and show the disagreement rather than averaging it away.
  • A local layer trained on local observations. A downscaling and bias-correction model for Banderas Bay, trained on the mesonet and radar the partnership installs, that turns a global forecast into wind and rain at the hotel strip, the marina, the river mouths and the hillsides. This is a small model and a small team; the data is the hard part.
  • Verification as a product, not an afterthought. A running scorecard like section 6, published by the SMN during the storm, so that forecasters and the public can see which guidance has been right for this storm and weight it accordingly. NOAA's own public page on AI hurricane forecasting says that AI systems "require one to constantly understand why the model generated the result that it produced"; a scorecard is how a forecaster does that at 3 am.
  • Probabilities first. The one product that carried the uncertainty honestly all week was the NHC's wind-speed probability table: 56–76% is a warning and not a promise, and it fell to 8% nine hours before the closest pass. It was also the product residents were least likely to notice; our own graphics carried it as a line under the building numbers. The deterministic line and the cone should be shown as what they are, one scenario inside a distribution.

10.4 The observing network behind the forecast

The federal auditor's report on the 2024 budget year records 85 of 387 observing systems out of service for lack of maintenance or storm damage, two of the eight weather radars down, 51 of 189 automatic stations down, and no new observatory or automatic station installed during the year. Conagua itself acknowledged in 2020 that the radar network had passed its useful life and that a 25-million-peso mobile X-band radar had been left out of the budget. No SMN radar in Jalisco or Nayarit could be found (the SMN's own radar page could not be checked); the nearest SMN radar known is Cuyutlán in Colima, about 225 km to the south-south-east, a 1994 unit the WMO radar database lists as on stand-by, and the next sites are far to the north (El Palmito in the Durango sierra, about 570 km; Guasave in Sinaloa). The Universidad de Guadalajara's own radar, about 200 km away across the Sierra, is described by the university as detecting storms reliably out to about 120 km, which does not reach the bay. Over the water the picture is thinner still. The US operations plan requires routine aircraft fixes for Atlantic and Gulf storms and says only that eastern Pacific missions "may be tasked when necessary"; for Lidia in 2023 that meant two flights and four center fixes, the last five hours before a category-4 landfall about 65 km from this city, and no coastal wind observation at all near the landfall point. In 2025 the squadron flew two eastern Pacific storms against nine in the Atlantic. This is not a criticism of the NHC, which forecasts for 28 countries with reconnaissance it does not control (the Air Force Reserve squadron and NOAA's own aircraft), tasked in the eastern Pacific only when necessary; that is the reason intensity at this coast rests on satellite estimates.

What is encouraging is that the ocean side has started to move. The Apixqui programme (CICESE, UNAM, the Navy and the SMN, funded by the science ministry since 2025) is deploying gliders and buoys in the Pacific, ran four glider missions off Guerrero last season, sampled Priscilla, and plans a radiosonde station; its stated motivation is Otis. A partnership on this coast should attach itself to that programme rather than duplicate it: Banderas Bay and the shelf off Cabo Corrientes are an obvious place for its buoys.

10.5 People and training

Two kinds of training are needed and they are usually confused. The first is the training of models, which needs data: the gridded AI models are trained on reanalysis at 28 km (DeepMind's cyclone model also on a database of about 5,000 historical storms), and the gridded models' known weaknesses (smoothed cores, collapsed intensity) are weaknesses of that training set. The local layer proposed above needs its own training set, which means two or three seasons of measurements in the bay before it is worth much. That is an argument for installing the instruments first and the models second, and for starting now.

The second is the training of people. The NHC runs a two-week workshop in English and Spanish for the region's government meteorologists every year; the WMO and the SMN agreed in 2020 to work on the SMN's technical staff development; neither is specific to AI guidance or to building-scale products. What the week showed is that a forecaster, a civil protection officer and a hotel manager each need to be able to read a probability, an ensemble spread and a verification table, and to say in plain words which model to believe tonight and why. That is a short course, not a degree, and it should be taught jointly to the SMN, the state unit (UEPCBJ), the municipal civil protection unit and the hotel association's emergency coordinators before each season, with the previous season's storms as the material. The partnership should also fund three full-time posts at a local university group (the Universidad de Guadalajara's meteorology institute, or CICESE through Apixqui) whose job during a storm is to run the verification, the local model and the resident channel, and whose job the rest of the year is to make the products better. The most useful observation of this storm came from a resident who said it was not raining; a WhatsApp group of thirty people with a view of the bay, a trained moderator and a rule that every report carries a time and a place is a legitimate instrument and costs nothing.

10.6 A realistic public-private partnership

The principle has to be stated first, because it is where such partnerships go wrong: the forecast and the warning stay public. The SMN is the official source, the national civil protection coordination issues the SIAT-CT alert, the state and municipal units act on it, and nothing a private partner produces replaces any of that. The WMO's 2019 Geneva Declaration says the same: the national services remain the core of observation and analysis, and the private sector's growing role should be organised around them. What a partnership adds is what the public side does not have the budget or the mandate for: instruments on private property, computing, product design, distribution to visitors, and money that does not depend on the next federal budget. Money, and where it realistically comes from. The order of magnitude matters more than the precision, so here is mine, to be costed properly: one dual-polarisation X-band radar sited on the bay, about 25–30 million pesos installed (Conagua's own 2020 figure for a mobile unit was 25 million); 30–50 coastal stations at roughly 150,000–250,000 pesos each including telemetry and three years of maintenance; two or three buoys through Apixqui; computing in the low hundreds of thousands of pesos a year; a technical team of six to eight people (the three university posts, a forecaster seconded from the SMN during the season, a designer-translator, and field staff for the stations); and the training programme. Over three seasons that is in the range of 70–120 million pesos (about US$4–7 million), comparable to what the SMN's budget programme for meteorological and hydrological systems spent in all of 2024 (97.5 million pesos) and a fifth to a third of one percent of the insured loss from Otis alone (35.5 billion pesos). The sources that could carry it already exist:

  • The lodging tax and a coastal trust. Quintana Roo pays for its reef-and-beach parametric insurance through a trust fed by hotel-tax revenue, created by the state, the hotel industry and The Nature Conservancy; it paid out after Hurricane Delta. A Banderas Bay trust on the same model, drawing a small share of the state lodging taxes, would fund the operating budget without a new line in the federal budget.
  • The hotel and tourism sector directly. The hotel association reported more than 12,000 visitors in the city at about 50% occupancy; hotels activated their protocols under the official watch and warning, keeping guests in interior safe areas, offering rebooking and in some cases moving guests to Guadalajara by road. The sector's interest in a better forecast is not charitable.
  • Insurers and the sovereign programme. Mexico holds about US$1.17 billion of parametric protection, including US$595 million of World Bank catastrophe bonds, one of them specific to Pacific hurricanes; Simon's lowest pressure crossed that bond's pressure threshold while its track points stayed just outside the trigger zones, "a very close call" in the words of one analyst. Parametric products are priced on observations, and the industry has a direct interest in a coast that can measure what hits it. The NOAA model of buying private observations under contract (US$6.4 million in two radio-occultation awards announced in August 2026) is the template for the public side paying for data rather than hardware.
  • Technology partners in kind. The Met Office–Microsoft supercomputer and the NHC–DeepMind arrangement show the two forms this takes: paid infrastructure, and model access with engineering time. Both are realistic at this scale; neither should be allowed to own the data or the product.
  • Development finance for the public core. The World Bank approved US$105 million for the SMN's last modernisation in 2012, and 95% of it was cancelled in 2016 (section 11.1); the IDB and WMO's SOFF fund observations in the region, though SOFF prioritises least-developed and small-island states and Mexico is not among its beneficiaries. A state-level loan or an IDB technical cooperation for the Jalisco–Nayarit coast is plausible; a national programme is not what this proposal needs to wait for.

Governance. A fideicomiso with a small board (state civil protection, the SMN, the municipality, the hotel association, the university, one insurer, one technology partner), an open-data rule for every observation and every forecast product, a published verification after every storm, and an annual review with the NHC arranged through the SMN, which represents Mexico on the WMO regional hurricane committee. The private partners get credit, data access and a seat; they do not get to issue a warning, and the agreement should say so in its first article.

10.8 What would make it fail

  • Treating it as a technology project. The radar and the models are the easy part. Maintenance budgets, a duty roster, and an agreement about who may say what during a storm are what decide whether it works in year three.
  • A private forecast competing with the official one. Two voices during a hurricane is worse than one bad one. Everything the partnership produces must be issued under the public authority or clearly labelled as guidance to it.
  • Closed data. If the observations or the products are proprietary, the SMN cannot use them, the NHC cannot verify against them and the next storm's lessons are lost. Open by default, with the trust as custodian.
  • Over-trusting the AI models after a good year. DeepMind's model won this storm and the 2025 season on track; its intensity skill is shorter-range, its runs have been erratic in at least one documented case, and it was unavailable in real time for part of 2025. The scorecard is the protection: weight by verified skill, every storm, not by reputation.
  • Building for Vallarta only. The same gap exists at Manzanillo, Barra de Navidad, San Blas and Mazatlán, and the storms do not choose. The products, the code and the course should be written to be copied.

10.9 The first ninety days

Convene the hotel association, the municipal and state civil protection units, the SMN's Pacific desk and the Universidad de Guadalajara's meteorology institute before the end of the year, with Simon's record (this report) as the agenda. Agree the principle in section 10.6. Ask Apixqui where its next buoys go. Price the stations and the radar. Draft the trust. Put the first twenty stations on roofs by May 2027, and run the scorecard and the building sheets, under the SMN's name, for the first storm of the season.
