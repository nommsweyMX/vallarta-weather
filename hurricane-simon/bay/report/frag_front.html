Hurricane Simon and Puerto Vallarta: what was forecast, what happened, and what coastal early warning needs next

Every forecast made for the city during the storm, set against the outcome; a verification of 25 physics, AI and ensemble models; a reply to the uploaded analysis Comprehensive Verification and Theoretical Analysis of Dynamical and AI-Driven Tropical Cyclone Forecasting; a proposal for how a public-private partnership could give this coast the forecasting it will need as the climate and the models change; and an honest look at where Red Hat and IBM could fit in it.

Based on NHC advisories 1–20A (last: 6 am CST Monday) · SMN aviso 40 (6 am CST) · ATCF decks to Monday 6 am · ECMWF and NOAA open data · NOAA/CIRA AI-model archive · METAR MMPR to Monday 8 am · GOES-18 · residents' reports
Not an official forecast. Follow the SMN, the NHC and Protección Civil. Emergencies: 911. Everything here is an independent reading of public data; it is meant to help you interpret the official products, not replace them.

1. The short version

  • What happened in Vallarta: no damaging weather. Simon's center passed its closest point to the city, 120 km to the south-west, around midnight Sunday as a 95-kt hurricane, then stalled. The airport reported winds of 0–4 kt all night and light rain on and off; residents in the city reported calm and little rain. The NHC says hurricane conditions were likely occurring on the coast between Playa Pérula and Cabo Corrientes; Puerto Vallarta itself remains inside the NHC and SMN hurricane warning area (Playa Pérula to El Roblito) as of 6 am Monday, and the sea is the hazard that is certain.
  • What was forecast. From Thursday morning to Saturday night (advisories 5 to 15) the NHC line put the center within 20 km of the city on ten of those eleven advisories (41 km on advisory 6), as a hurricane of category 1 to 3 at that point on most of them. The line moved to 50 km on Sunday 3 am, 74 km at 9 am and 101 km at 3 pm; the real closest pass was 120 km. The NHC's own odds of tropical-storm-force wind in the city were 56–76% from Friday to Sunday morning and dropped to 8% at 3 pm Sunday.
  • What our resident graphics said. The sets built on Sunday morning gave the three buildings gusts of 68–83 km/h; the 3 pm set gave 47–61 km/h; the first set also gave 200–250 mm of rain, scaled to the SMN regional figure, before it was corrected the same day on a ground report that it was not raining. Earlier products were higher still: a Saturday-morning hour-by-hour map put 145 km/h sustained wind on the Amapas house for Sunday afternoon with the center 9 km away, and a Saturday-night hour-by-hour table told El Pitillal not to go out from 10 pm Sunday to 4 am Monday (section 4.1). None of this happened. The morning numbers came from the NHC's own 56% probability of tropical-storm-force wind, converted into the wind with a 50% chance and scaled to each building; the 3 pm numbers came from the NHC line and its open-water wind envelope. Neither came from the models that were right.
  • Which models were right. Over the two days before the pass, the best position forecasts came from Google DeepMind's cyclone model (mean error 33 km), the NHC's corrected consensus (38 km) and the NHC official forecast (47 km). The US regional hurricane models HAFS-A, HAFS-B, HMON and COAMPS-TC carried the center 100–170 km off, mostly to the north; HWRF and UKMET did no better than the global models; the GFS-driven AI models did as badly on the leads they could be checked. Every gridded AI model collapsed Simon's intensity to a tropical storm while it stayed a category 4.
  • What comes next. Advisory 20A (Monday 6 am): the center is 80 km west-southwest of Cabo Corrientes, drifting northwest at 4 km/h; it is forecast to move slowly away from the bay, then turn northeast toward the Nayarit–Sinaloa coast around Wednesday night as a weakening hurricane. The NHC's Vallarta probabilities are now below the 3% display threshold. The SMN (aviso 40) forecasts 50–75 mm for western Jalisco and the Nayarit coast and waves of 4–5 m on the Jalisco coast, decreasing through the day. The sea remains the hazard that is certain.
  • On the uploaded analysis. Its physics and its account of the AI intensity problem hold up and Simon confirmed them. Its model-verification table does not: the numbers have no source, and several contradict the record. It also omits the one model that actually won.
  • What this storm asks for. The gap was not in the global models; it was in everything between the global grid and a window in Amapas: no radar over Banderas Bay, no public verification of the forecast as it ran, no local wind or rain model trained on local observations, and a cone that cannot carry probability. Section 10 sets out a public-private partnership that could close that gap in three seasons, with the roles, the money and the measures of success; section 11 asks where a technology partner such as Red Hat and IBM fits, and answers that the gap is not AI knowledge but the platform, the devices, the people and the money that keep a public service running.

2. Monday morning: where Simon is and what is forecast

NHC advisory 20A (6 am CST Monday): center 20.1N 106.4W, 100 kt (115 mph), 958 mb, drifting northwest at 4 km/h, 80 km west-southwest of Cabo Corrientes. The forecast (advisory 20): 20.6N 106.7W and 90 kt by noon Monday, 21.2N 107.1W and 85 kt by midnight, 21.8N 107.3W and 80 kt by noon Tuesday, 80 kt through Wednesday noon near 22.9N 107.0W, then inland near 23.4N 106.6W as a 70-kt hurricane around midnight Wednesday and dissipating over the Sierra Madre by Friday. The discussion notes a higher-than-normal track uncertainty while the storm sits between two ridges, and that the latest line was moved slightly west "after considering some of the latest AI-based guidance". Puerto Vallarta no longer appears in the wind-speed probability table, which lists a location only when its chance of tropical-storm-force wind reaches 3%. The hurricane warning from Playa Pérula to El Roblito still covers the city and the whole bay.

SMN aviso 40 (6 am CST): very heavy point rainfall (50–75 mm) in western Jalisco, the Nayarit coast and west, and southern Sinaloa; gusts of 90–100 km/h and waves of 4–5 m on the Jalisco coast, decreasing through the day; gusts of 50–60 km/h and waves of 2.5–3.5 m on the coasts of Nayarit and central and southern Sinaloa. The NHC's rainfall statement keeps 2–4 in (50–100 mm) of additional rain across coastal southwest and west-central Mexico, isolated 6–8 in in Jalisco, and isolated storm totals of 30 in over parts of Michoacán, Colima and Jalisco.

For the city today: the wind the parametric field gives the three buildings is now 37–47 km/h in gusts from the south-east, the sea is the remaining hazard, and the rain picture changes if the storm lingers to the west long enough for the wind to turn onshore (the consensus puts that on Tuesday morning). 3. What was forecast, advisory by advisory, and what happened

The NHC issued twenty advisories for Simon between Wednesday morning and Monday morning. Figure 2 draws the first 72 hours of every forecast line over the real track; Figure 3 reduces each line to the one number a resident of Vallarta cares about, how close it would come, and the intensity at that point; Figure 4 is the NHC's own probability of damaging wind in the city, advisory by advisory.

The sequence has three phases. Wednesday to Saturday night (advisories 1–15): the line came within 6–57 km of the city, within 20 km on ten of those fifteen advisories, with 48–103 kt at the closest point (68–103 kt leaving out advisory 6); the cone, drawn around that line, covered the whole bay. Sunday morning (advisories 16–17): as the storm held a north-northwest heading parallel to the coast instead of bending north-northeast, the line moved to 50 and then 74 km. Sunday 3 pm (advisory 18): 101 km, and the tropical-storm probability fell from 56% to 8% in one step. What happened: 120 km, farther than any advisory had forecast, and then a stall that kept the center 120–140 km from the city through Monday morning.

Two things are worth saying plainly. First, the official line was 45–115 km too close to the city on every advisory from 1 to 17. At two to three days out that is about the NHC's average 72-hour track error for this basin (97 km in 2025); at one to two days it was larger than usual. The NHC's discussions explain the placement: the forecasters kept the line on the eastern side of the guidance because storms here have historically tracked east of the models, several global and regional models agreed on a coastal approach, and the discussions said repeatedly not to focus on the exact line. Second, the probabilities were more honest than the line: a 56–76% chance of tropical-storm-force wind is not a promise, and a reader who had them would have expected an event forecast at those odds to miss one time in three or four. It missed. The line and the cone, which are what got redistributed, offered no way to see that; the probabilities and the discussions did, to those who read them.

4. Every product issued for the city, against the outcome

4.1 Saturday: the earlier products

Four products were issued for the city before the Sunday building sets, on advisories 12, 14 and 15 (Saturday 3 am, 3 pm and 9 pm). They are reproduced as issued in Appendix A0, and Figure 5b puts all of them, with the Sunday and Monday sets, on one time axis against the one number that was measured.

  • Hour-by-hour maps, advisory 12 (Saturday 3 am). Two addresses, the house on Calle Cafeto in Amapas and 5 de Mayo y España in El Pitillal, with a wind interpolated from the NHC forecast points. For Sunday 4 pm they gave 90 mph (145 km/h) sustained and 115 mph (185 km/h) gusts with the center "5 nm south", and both addresses "extreme"; for Sunday 10 pm, 40 mph sustained with the center 89 km north, and El Pitillal still "extreme" for the river crest. Their own footer says the zones were judgment, not official polygons.
  • Model snapshot, advisory 14 (Saturday 5:36 pm). Peak sustained wind 39–58 mph (63–93 km/h) as an "NHC median bracket", 43–67 mph (69–108 km/h) at a seventh floor as a "height-only scenario", a 72-hour rain blend of 70 mm with a model range of 2.5–175 mm, 1.4 m offshore waves, the NHC odds of 63/22/4%, and river flooding and surge marked "unresolved".
  • The two videos, advisory 15 (Saturday 8:30 pm). Explainers on category, rain, terrain, waves and height, then the forecast: the center passing near Vallarta around midnight Sunday to Monday, about 15 km west, with the NHC's 75/37/15% odds; tropical-storm-force wind from Sunday afternoon; a 12-hour margin of about 45 km; and the advice to "go by the odds and the warnings, not the line".
  • Hour-by-hour table and card for El Pitillal, advisory 15 (Saturday 9 pm). Five classes from "poco" to "no salir" (do not go out) for wind, rain and river, hour by hour: it starts Sunday 2 pm, the worst is 10 pm Sunday to 4 am Monday, it is over by 9 am Monday, and the river stays high until Monday afternoon.

Read together, the Saturday products say three things. First, the advisory-12 maps are the worst product of the week and the most instructive: they put the hurricane's own wind on a house because the interpolated line passed 9 km from it. A center wind is not a building's wind, and a line 9 km from a city two days out is a line with a 100-km error bar (section 3); this report's rule that the storm's center wind is never presented as Vallarta's wind comes from that product. Second, the model snapshot was the most careful product of the week: it called its wind a median bracket, showed five rain models disagreeing by a factor of seventy, declined to put a number on surge or the river, and was still too high, because the bracket came from the NHC's 63%. Third, the videos and the El Pitillal table did what section 9 recommends, odds first and the line as one scenario, and still told people to expect a bad night, because the odds they carried were the NHC's 75%: "no salir" from 10 pm Sunday to 4 am Monday is a defensible reading of a 75% chance of tropical-storm-force wind, and it was wrong. The record of the week is not that the later products were careful and the earlier ones careless. It is that every product that followed the official line and the official odds was too high, by an amount that shrank with each advisory and never reached zero until the storm had passed.

4.2 Sunday and Monday: the building sets

Three versions of the resident graphics were produced on Sunday (Appendix A has every page of each, in English and on a white background for this report): the first set at 9:10 am on advisory 17, a corrected set at 1 pm after a resident reported that it was not raining, and the 3 pm set on advisory 18. A fourth set, on advisory 20, is Figure 1 and Appendix A4. Figure 5a reduces the four to what they said for the three buildings and sets that against the airport record.

What went wrong, and why. The two Sunday-morning sets did not take their wind from the NHC line at all. They took it from the NHC's probabilities: a 56% chance of tropical-storm-force wind was converted into the wind the city had a 50% chance of reaching (36 kt sustained), and that was scaled to each building by its exposure (0.70 to 0.89); the line-and-radii field gave more (42 kt) and was capped by it. In other words, the morning numbers were the NHC's own odds, read as a median. The 3 pm set was the first in which the line and the radii set the number (26 kt sustained, below the probability cap), and it was still too high, because the official 34-kt radius in the north-east quadrant was 130–150 km on Sunday morning and 93 km at 3 pm, so any line passing within that distance put tropical-storm-force gusts over the bay, and the radius is an open-water, all-quadrant envelope that says nothing about the lee of the Sierra Madre. The line itself was 45–115 km too close until Sunday afternoon. The rain error was simpler and worse: the first set multiplied the satellite estimate by 5.8 to match the SMN's "more than 250 mm", a regional maximum for "Jalisco (coast and west)" that in practice describes the windward terrain rather than the city (our reading, section 5). It was corrected within four hours because one resident said it was not raining, which is the most useful observation this storm produced. A third error surfaced on Monday morning and is worth recording because it is exactly the kind a verification step catches: the satellite centre-finder, which supplies the last four hours of observed positions to the bay fields, locked onto cold cloud tops over the Sierra and placed Simon's centre near Guadalajara; the first Monday build carried that error (winds from the north-west) until the fixes were checked against the NHC position and replaced. Figure 1 and Appendix A4 are the corrected set.

What went right. The model scorecard (section 6) identified on Sunday morning which models to trust and which not, and the forecasts it rated best kept the center 100 km or more from the city; the satellite rain estimate, left unscaled, gave the city under 10 mm, consistent with the airport's light rain on and off (its precipitation groups are inconsistent, 4–35 mm per six hours, and there is no reliable gauge total); the timing of the closest pass (around midnight) was right; the sea warnings could not be checked from here, since no wave or water-level observation for the bay was available and the only report is that the malecón was closed. The graphics also carried the NHC's probabilities on every page; from 3 pm those said "unlikely" (8%) while the building numbers still said "strong", and in the morning the 56% they showed was, honestly read, a coin toss.

The lesson for the design. A resident product should lead with the probabilities and show the deterministic wind as the worst plausible case, labelled as such; it should never scale a satellite estimate to a regional forecast; it should never put the wind at the storm's center on an address because the forecast line passes nearby; and it needs a ground-truth channel, even a WhatsApp group of ten people with a view of the bay, wired into the next update. Section 10 turns that into a proposal.
