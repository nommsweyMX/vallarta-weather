Hurricane Simon and Puerto Vallarta: what was forecast, what happened, and what coastal early warning needs next

Every forecast made for the city during the storm, set against the outcome; a verification of 25 physics, AI and ensemble models; a reply to the uploaded analysis Comprehensive Verification and Theoretical Analysis of Dynamical and AI-Driven Tropical Cyclone Forecasting; and a proposal for how a public-private partnership could give this coast the forecasting it will need as the climate and the models change.

Based on NHC advisories 1–20A (last: 6 am CST Monday) · SMN aviso 40 (6 am CST) · ATCF decks to Monday 6 am · ECMWF and NOAA open data · NOAA/CIRA AI-model archive · METAR MMPR to Monday 8 am · GOES-18 · residents' reports
Not an official forecast. Follow the SMN, the NHC and Protección Civil. Emergencies: 911. Everything here is an independent reading of public data; it is meant to help you interpret the official products, not replace them.

1. The short version

  • What happened in Vallarta: nothing. Simon's center passed its closest point to the city, 120 km to the south-west, around midnight Sunday as a 95-kt hurricane, then stalled. The airport reported winds of 0–4 kt all night and light rain on and off; residents in the city reported calm and little rain. The eastern eyewall brushed the Jalisco coast between Playa Pérula and Cabo Corrientes, where the hurricane warning remains.
  • What was forecast. From Thursday morning to Saturday night (advisories 5 to 15) the NHC line put the center within 20 km of the city on ten of those eleven advisories (41 km on advisory 6), as a hurricane of category 1 to 3 at that point on most of them. The line moved to 50 km on Sunday 3 am, 74 km at 9 am and 101 km at 3 pm; the real closest pass was 120 km. The NHC's own odds of tropical-storm-force wind in the city were 56–76% from Friday to Sunday morning and dropped to 8% at 3 pm Sunday.
  • What our resident graphics said. The sets built on Sunday morning gave the three buildings gusts of 68–83 km/h; the 3 pm set gave 47–61 km/h; the first set also gave 200–250 mm of rain, scaled to the SMN regional figure, before it was corrected the same day on a ground report that it was not raining. None of this happened. The morning numbers came from the NHC's own 56% probability of tropical-storm-force wind, converted into the wind with a 50% chance and scaled to each building; the 3 pm numbers came from the NHC line and its open-water wind envelope. Neither came from the models that were right.
  • Which models were right. Over the two days before the pass, the best position forecasts came from Google DeepMind's cyclone model (mean error 33 km), the NHC's corrected consensus (38 km) and the NHC official forecast (47 km). The US regional hurricane models and UKMET carried the center about 150 km too far north; the GFS-driven AI models did as badly on the leads they could be checked. Every gridded AI model collapsed Simon's intensity to a tropical storm while it stayed a category 4.
  • What comes next. Advisory 20A (Monday 6 am): the center is 80 km west-southwest of Cabo Corrientes, drifting northwest at 4 km/h; it is forecast to move slowly away from the bay, then turn northeast toward the Nayarit–Sinaloa coast around Wednesday night as a weakening hurricane. The NHC's Vallarta probabilities are now below the 3% display threshold. The SMN (aviso 40) forecasts 50–75 mm for western Jalisco and the Nayarit coast and waves of 4–5 m on the Jalisco coast, decreasing through the day. The sea remains the hazard that is certain.
  • On the uploaded analysis. Its physics and its account of the AI intensity problem hold up and Simon confirmed them. Its model-verification table does not: the numbers have no source, and several contradict the record. It also omits the one model that actually won.
  • What this storm asks for. The gap was not in the global models; it was in everything between the global grid and a window in Amapas: no radar over Banderas Bay, no public verification of the forecast as it ran, no local wind or rain model trained on local observations, and a cone that cannot carry probability. Section 11 sets out a public-private partnership that could close that gap in three seasons, with the roles, the money and the measures of success.

2. Monday morning: where Simon is and what is forecast

NHC advisory 20A (6 am CST Monday): center 20.1N 106.4W, 100 kt (115 mph), 958 mb, drifting northwest at 4 km/h, 80 km west-southwest of Cabo Corrientes. The forecast (advisory 20): 20.6N 106.7W and 90 kt by noon Monday, 21.2N 107.1W and 85 kt by midnight, 21.8N 107.3W and 80 kt by noon Tuesday, 80 kt through Wednesday noon near 22.9N 107.0W, then inland near 23.4N 106.6W as a 70-kt hurricane around midnight Wednesday and dissipating over the Sierra Madre by Friday. The discussion notes a higher-than-normal track uncertainty while the storm sits between two ridges, and that the latest line was moved slightly west "after considering some of the latest AI-based guidance". Puerto Vallarta no longer appears in the wind-speed probability table, which lists a location only when its chance of tropical-storm-force wind reaches 3%.

3. What was forecast, advisory by advisory, and what happened

The NHC issued twenty advisories for Simon between Wednesday morning and Monday morning. Figure 2 draws the first 72 hours of every forecast line over the real track; Figure 3 reduces each line to the one number a resident of Vallarta cares about, how close it would come, and the intensity at that point; Figure 4 is the NHC's own probability of damaging wind in the city, advisory by advisory.

The sequence has three phases. Wednesday to Saturday night (advisories 1–15): the line came within 6–57 km of the city, within 20 km on ten of those fifteen advisories, with 48–103 kt at the closest point (68–103 kt leaving out advisory 6); the cone, drawn around that line, covered the whole bay. Sunday morning (advisories 16–17): as the storm held a north-northwest heading parallel to the coast instead of bending north-northeast, the line moved to 50 and then 74 km. Sunday 3 pm (advisory 18): 101 km, and the tropical-storm probability fell from 56% to 8% in one step. What happened: 120 km, farther than any advisory had forecast, and then a stall that kept the center 120–140 km from the city through Monday morning.

Two things are worth saying plainly. First, the NHC's forecast was wrong in the direction that mattered for four days, and it was wrong because the regional hurricane models it relies on for structure were wrong in that direction (section 6). Second, its probabilities were more honest than its line: a 56–76% chance of tropical-storm-force wind is not a promise, and a reader who had the probabilities would have expected an event forecast at those odds to miss one time in three or four. It missed. The line and the cone offered no way to see that.

4. The resident graphics against the outcome

Three versions of the resident graphics were produced on Sunday (Appendix A has every page of each, in English and on a white background for this report): the first set at 9:10 am on advisory 17, a corrected set at 1 pm after a resident reported that it was not raining, and the 3 pm set on advisory 18. A fourth set, on advisory 20, is Figure 1 and Appendix A4. Figure 5 reduces the four to what they said for the three buildings and sets that against the airport record.

What went wrong, and why. The two Sunday-morning sets did not take their wind from the NHC line at all. They took it from the NHC's probabilities: a 56% chance of tropical-storm-force wind was converted into the wind the city had a 50% chance of reaching (36 kt sustained), and that was scaled to each building by its exposure (0.70 to 0.89); the line-and-radii field gave more (42 kt) and was capped by it. In other words, the morning numbers were the NHC's own odds, read as a median. The 3 pm set was the first in which the line and the radii set the number (26 kt sustained, below the probability cap), and it was still too high, because the official 34-kt radius in the north-east quadrant was 130–150 km on Sunday morning and 93 km at 3 pm, so any line passing within that distance put tropical-storm-force gusts over the bay, and the radius is an open-water, all-quadrant envelope that says nothing about the lee of the Sierra Madre. The line itself was 45–115 km too close until Sunday afternoon. The rain error was simpler and worse: the first set multiplied the satellite estimate by 5.8 to match the SMN's "more than 250 mm", a regional maximum for "Jalisco (coast and west)" that in practice describes the windward terrain rather than the city (our reading, section 5). It was corrected within four hours because one resident said it was not raining, which is the most useful observation this storm produced. A third error surfaced on Monday morning and is worth recording because it is exactly the kind a verification step catches: the satellite centre-finder, which supplies the last four hours of observed positions to the bay fields, locked onto cold cloud tops over the Sierra and placed Simon's centre near Guadalajara; the first Monday build carried that error (winds from the north-west) until the fixes were checked against the NHC position and replaced. Figure 1 and Appendix A4 are the corrected set.

What went right. The model scorecard (section 6) identified on Sunday morning which models to trust and which not, and the forecasts it rated best kept the center 100 km or more from the city; the satellite rain estimate, left unscaled, gave the city under 10 mm, consistent with the airport's light rain on and off (its precipitation groups are inconsistent, 4–35 mm per six hours, and there is no reliable gauge total); the timing of the closest pass (around midnight) was right; and the sea warnings were right. The graphics also carried the NHC's probabilities on every page; from 3 pm those said "unlikely" (8%) while the building numbers still said "strong", and in the morning the 56% they showed was, honestly read, a coin toss.

The lesson for the design. A resident product should lead with the probabilities and show the deterministic wind as the worst plausible case, labelled as such; it should never scale a satellite estimate to a regional forecast; and it needs a ground-truth channel, even a WhatsApp group of ten people with a view of the bay, wired into the next update. Section 10 turns that into a proposal.
