11. Where Red Hat and IBM fit, and where they do not

The question this section answers was put to me directly: is there something Red Hat and IBM can do here, or is the gap simply that the weather service has the domain knowledge and lacks the AI knowledge? The short answer is that the gap is not AI knowledge, and that a technology partner is useful in exactly the places where this coast's last modernisation failed. The long answer follows, with the same rule as section 10: every claim about what the two companies have is sourced, and where I am guessing it says so.

11.1 The gap is not AI knowledge

Four facts settle this. First, the model that won this storm is public, and so are the others a service would want to run. ECMWF put the weights of its AI model on Hugging Face in December 2024 under a licence that allows any use, and its checkpoint is 1.2 GB; Google DeepMind released the code and weights of the cyclone model that beat the NHC's own track forecast in 2025 on 6 August 2026, under Apache 2.0 for the code and CC BY 4.0 for the weights; the NASA-IBM Prithvi WxC model has been open since September 2024 and its downscaling variant is published under the same licence. Second, running them is cheap. DeepMind's global forecast runs in under a minute on one accelerator; its full cyclone model needs a single H100-class GPU and its smaller version runs on a card a decade old; the initial conditions come free from ECMWF's open data and from NOAA's GFS bucket on AWS, which needs no account. Third, the people who forecast hurricanes for a living do not treat this as a knowledge transfer from the AI side. The NHC runs DeepMind's forecasts under a cooperative agreement signed in July 2025 in which Google provides the forecasts and hosts the display and NOAA evaluates and integrates them; its director's description is that "there is a human forecaster taking all of that guidance". The WMO's Executive Council said the same in June 2025 and its Congress repeated it in October: AI must complement, not replace, the existing methods, the national service stays the single authoritative voice, and the real risk is that AI widens the gap between members that can run it and members that cannot.

Fourth, and closest to home, is what happened the last time money arrived to modernise the SMN. The World Bank's MoMet project, a US$105 million loan signed in 2012 to strengthen "the human resource, institutional, and infrastructural capacity" of the service, was cancelled at Mexico's request in 2016 with US$4.7 million of the US$105 million disbursed, 4.7% in the Bank's own count. The Bank's last status report rated progress "Unsatisfactory", raised the institutional-capacity risk to "High", and counted 61 of 198 automatic-station contracts awarded and none of the four pilot early-warning systems delivered. On the Bank's own record (an Unsatisfactory rating, institutional-capacity risk raised to High, fiduciary risk Substantial, 61 of 198 station contracts awarded), my reading is that the project failed on procurement and institutional capacity, not on meteorology or software. Section 10.4 shows the result ten years later: a network a quarter out of service and no new station installed in 2024. What the SMN lacks, on that reading, is not how a transformer works. It is a partner that can keep a platform, a fleet of devices and a training pipeline running for years, inside Mexican procurement rules, and hand it over working.

11.2 What IBM actually has today

It is important to start with what IBM no longer has. IBM sold The Weather Company to Francisco Partners (announced 22 August 2023, closed 31 January 2024, US$1.1 billion including contingent payments), and with it the forecasting business, the Weather Channel properties and the GRAF model, which is now marketed as proprietary to The Weather Company, which is training a new kilometre-scale AI model on its output with NVIDIA. IBM kept the Environmental Intelligence Suite, a data-and-alerts product that still runs on Weather Company data (its API changelog was still being updated in 2026 and it is on the UK government's G-Cloud framework at £57,324 a licence a year), but as of this week IBM's own product pages for it redirect to its emissions-accounting software. IBM is not a forecaster, and it should not be asked to be one. Its history is also instructive: Deep Thunder, IBM Research's hyperlocal model, ran a 1-km forecast for Rio de Janeiro's operations centre from 2011 as the showpiece of a US$14 million smart-city partnership after the 2010 floods; by 2023 the partnership had ended, for reasons neither side has explained; the centre's director said no IBM products were left; Gaffney and Robertson (2018) found its cameras clustered in wealthier districts, and Gaffney, interviewed in 2023, called the approach "very reactive, not proactive"; no quantitative evaluation of its warnings could be found. That is the pattern a Vallarta partnership must avoid, and section 11.5 says how.

What IBM does have is research-grade and open. Prithvi WxC (IBM Research with NASA, September 2024) is a 2.3-billion-parameter weather foundation model trained on 40 years of the MERRA-2 reanalysis, released under a permissive licence; its paper reports a 64-km mean track error on Hurricane Ida's reanalysis-driven rollout against 200–260 km for FourCastNet, but it works on a 55-km grid, its skill falls below Pangu-Weather after about 66 hours, its intensity forecasts are slightly worse than FourCastNet's, and no weather service is known to run it operationally. Its downscaling variant, granite-geospatial-wxc-downscaling, is more directly useful: it is documented taking Environment and Climate Change Canada's 15-km global wind forecasts to the 2.5-km grid of the Canadian regional model, which is a close precedent for the local layer section 10.3 asks for, built with a national service, with two differences: it is trained to reproduce the Canadian regional model's output rather than observations, and it is wind only and still at research stage. The Prithvi-EO-2.0 and TerraMind Earth-observation models (with NASA and ESA, Apache 2.0) have published flood-mapping fine-tunes, one of them demonstrated on Hurricane Helene; mapping what flooded after a storm is the verification layer for the rain products this report could not check. IBM Research and its co-authors say, in a 2025 conference abstract on ensembles of these models, that whether AI emulators "can be reliably deployed in all use cases (e.g., for the forecast of extreme scenarios)" is "still open". That is the right register for a partner.

IBM's programmes matter more than its models here. The IBM Impact Accelerator (the former Sustainability Accelerator) gives five organisations a year a two-year, no-cost engagement with IBM engineers, watsonx, Granite, IBM Cloud, Environmental Intelligence and Red Hat tools; IBM committed US$45 million over five years in 2024, its resilient-cities cohort, chosen in February 2025 from more than a hundred applicants, includes the World Food Programme's hazard-vulnerability maps and a hyperlocal extreme-heat warning system for hospitals, and each project is worth up to US$3 million. It is open to governments and universities. No storm- or flood-warning project and no weather-service partner has been in any cohort; the 2026 call (issued 4 February 2026, on the future of work and education) closed on 25 March, so the earliest entry would be the 2027 call. A Banderas Bay application would be new ground for the programme, which cuts both ways. IBM SkillsBuild is free, in Spanish, and IBM is already one of the companies inside Mexico's new public AI training centre, which runs free five-month tracks at TecNM campuses in ten cities, Nayarit among them. And IBM's Guadalajara campus, which marked fifty years in 2025 and is now a hybrid-cloud and AI hub with six centres of excellence, is about 200 km from the bay across the Sierra.

11.3 What Red Hat actually has today

Red Hat has no case study of a national weather service running forecasting or AI on OpenShift. The closest things on record are Linux and clustering for a regional Italian agency's forecast models for the 2006 Winter Olympics, virtualisation at Belgium's Royal Meteorological Institute in 2012, an API platform for a Japanese weather company in 2018, a 2023 military-simulation collaboration with BAE Systems and The Weather Company, and the fact that ECMWF's Bologna supercomputer runs Red Hat Enterprise Linux as an operating-system choice. That absence is itself an argument: this would be a reference case of a kind the company does not have.

What it has are the three layers the partnership in section 10 cannot buy from a weather vendor. OpenShift AI (now Red Hat AI 3) provides the model registry, versioned pipelines, GPU-scheduled training and model serving that a running scorecard and a retrained local layer need; the one documented forecasting precedent is Boston University's ecological forecasting system, moved from a supercomputer onto OpenShift AI in 2023, ingesting ECMWF reanalysis and autoscaling model runs, a smaller version of exactly this job. Red Hat Device Edge, with the MicroShift Kubernetes and the Edge Manager fleet tool previewed in May 2025, runs on two cores and two gigabytes, does delta updates over intermittent links with automatic rollback, and exposes serial ports and USB sensors to containers: the specification of a coastal weather-station gateway that must survive a season on a hotel roof. Open Innovation Labs is a four-to-twelve-week residency in which the customer's own engineers pair with Red Hat's; the WHO built its COVID-19 training platform for health workers that way, and it is the natural vehicle for the "people" layer in section 10.5, with SMN, state and municipal civil-protection engineers in the room. Red Hat also brings open-data governance it has practised: it committed eight engineers to the Linux Foundation's OS-Climate data commons in 2021, and its open-source programme office is the model for the trust's open-data rule.

Most relevant of all, Red Hat already runs inside the Mexican federal government at scale. The Instituto Mexicano del Seguro Social operates seven OpenShift clusters hosting 5,400 microservices and 140 applications that handle one to three billion requests a day, with OpenShift AI, Ansible and virtualisation being added in 2026 and the national digital-strategy office citing it as a model. Mexico's federal ICT policy of 2021, which cloud providers were still citing in 2024 (whether the 2025 digital agency has amended it was not checked), requires public institutions to prefer government data centres and to keep critical data on national territory, allowing public cloud only in justified cases; three hyperscalers now run regions in Querétaro, and IBM Cloud has none in Mexico. A platform that runs the same way on an SMN server, in a Querétaro region or on a university cluster is the kind those rules favour, and portable platforms of that kind, OpenShift among them, are what Red Hat sells.

11.4 The position: the open platform and operations partner

Put together, the realistic position for Red Hat and IBM is not "we have the AI the weather service needs". It is: the forecast and the warning stay with the SMN and civil protection; the models come from wherever they are best (ECMWF, DeepMind, NOAA, and IBM Research where it fits); and Red Hat and IBM supply the platform that runs them, the devices that feed them, the engineering discipline that keeps them running, the training that leaves people able to do it without them, and part of the money, in exchange for a reference case of a kind they do not have and a seat on a board they do not control. 11.5 What would make it go wrong

  • Becoming the forecaster. The Rio partnership had the vendor as integrator and its model as centrepiece, and ended with none of the vendor's products in the building. The forecast here is the SMN's, the models are open, and the agreement should say that the partnership can be unwound in a season with everything still running.
  • Confusing research models with operations. Prithvi WxC is not known to have been run operationally by any weather service (none was found in searches to 12 October 2026) and its own authors call reliability on extremes an open question. It belongs in the university group's research line, not on the forecaster's desk, until the scorecard says otherwise.
  • Closed anything. Open weights, open data, open code for the local layer, and a public verification after every storm. Red Hat's own business is the argument for this; it should be written into the agreement, not assumed.
  • Hosting outside the rules. Mexican policy prefers national territory and public infrastructure for critical data. The platform must run on-premises or in-country; the trust, not the vendor, chooses where.
  • Mistaking a residency for a roster. The MoMet project shows what happens when capacity is bought as a project and not kept as a budget. The partnership's measure of success in season 3 is that the SMN runs it alone for a storm.

11.6 The answer, in one paragraph

The weather service has the domain knowledge and, in its forecasters, more judgment about this coast than any model. The AI knowledge is in the open: the models, the weights, the initial conditions and the papers are public, and running them costs minutes on one machine. What is missing is everything that turns a public model into a public service that survives three seasons: devices that stay up, a platform that runs every cycle, a verification that is published, people trained to use the output and to say when not to, and money that does not depend on the next federal budget. Red Hat and IBM are not the answer to the first two questions and are a credible answer to the last five, provided the forecast stays public, the stack stays open, and the first thing they build is the public scorecard that, by Saturday, would have shown which models to discount (section 9), something no product in Appendix A0 had behind it.
