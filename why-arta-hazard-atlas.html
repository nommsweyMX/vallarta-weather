Tectonics & Monitoring · the deeper picture

How this coast is built — and how it's watched

You asked for the science behind the Bahía de Banderas. Here it is across four fronts: the plate setup, the networks on the ground, what satellites see, and the frontier research that gets oversold.

1 · The tectonic setting — a corner of three plates

Puerto Vallarta sits at one of the more complicated corners in the Americas: the Guadalajara triple junction, where the small, young Rivera plate, the larger Cocos plate, and the continental North American plate all meet. Beneath the bay, the Rivera plate dives under a detached crustal fragment called the Jalisco Block at a shallow angle (roughly 8–14°), and — being small and young — it subducts at only about half the rate of Cocos to the south.

The Jalisco Block itself is being torn away from mainland Mexico along three rifts (Tepic–Zacoalco, Colima, Chapala), drifting northwest at ~2 mm/yr. That rifting plus the subduction is why the region is criss-crossed with active structures — the Banderas Canyon (which appears to be pulling open west-to-east, continuing inland up the Río Pitillal valley), the Ipala Canyon, and the El Gordo graben.

Researchers split the local seismicity into three distinct zones: the subduction thrust offshore (the source of the M8 events), a shallow continental zone inland around Ameca–Amatlán, and the Bahía de Banderas zone itself, where the stress field is genuinely complex and not a simple plate boundary. That complexity is the reason your catalog shows events scattered across very different depths and styles.

2 · How it's monitored now

The dense local coverage that makes your SSN app show so many small events comes largely from the RESAJ (Red Sísmica de Jalisco) — an accelerometric telemetric network of ~30 stations, eleven of them carrying GNSS receivers, run from the SisVOc lab right in Puerto Vallarta. A single RESAJ study in 2015 located 683 local quakes in six months and caught swarms in both the bay and under Guadalajara.

Layered on top: TLALOCNet, a continuous GPS + meteorology network spanning Mexico for strain and atmospheric studies; and major offshore campaigns — the TSUJAL marine seismic survey (2014, a Spanish–Mexican–British effort) and the MARS "Mapping the Rivera Subduction" experiment with ocean-bottom seismometers — which imaged the slab geometry and the thin (~10 km) subducting plate beneath the bay.

3 · What the satellites see — locked and loading

This is the remote-sensing punchline, and it lines up with the Gap page. Combining Sentinel-1 InSAR (four descending tracks, 2016–2019.5) with the GNSS network, researchers mapped how tightly the plate interface is stuck. The Jalisco segment (roughly −106° to −104°E — i.e. your stretch of coast) comes out highly coupled, on the order of 70–100% locked.

The more telling detail: unlike the Guerrero and Oaxaca subduction zones farther southeast, which regularly release built-up stress through large slow-slip events (quiet, aseismic creep that shows up in GNSS/InSAR but not as quakes), the Jalisco interface shows no such large transients. It isn't bleeding off strain gently. It locks and loads. Satellites are effectively watching the coast quietly accumulate the slip deficit — measured as sufficient for an ~M8 since 1932 — that a future rupture will spend.

Why this matters for the read
A zone that creeps in slow-slip events is partly self-relieving. A zone that's strongly locked with no transients is storing its budget for an earthquake. Jalisco is the second kind — which is exactly what makes the Vallarta Gap's overdue status meaningful rather than alarmist.

4 · The space-weather frontier — interesting, not predictive

You flagged space-weather data, and it's a real and active field — but it's where honesty matters most, because it gets oversold. The idea, called lithosphere–atmosphere–ionosphere coupling (LAIC), is that stress in the crust before a big quake might perturb the upper atmosphere — measurable as Total Electron Content (TEC) anomalies in the ionosphere via the same GNSS signals used for geodesy, or by satellites like CSES and Swarm.

Studies do report TEC anomalies days before some M≥5.5 events, with detection rates around 47% in one 2025 statistical sample. The proposed mechanisms — radon release, ground-level electric fields, upward acoustic-gravity waves — are physically plausible.

But the critical reviews are blunt about the weakness: most of these studies fail to properly rule out actual space weather — geomagnetic storms and solar flares perturb the ionosphere far more strongly than any earthquake, and many papers don't filter for them, which undermines the causal claim. Detection is well under half, lead times are inconsistent, and there is no validated operational earthquake-prediction capability from any of it. Treat it as frontier research, not a working alarm.

The neat part
The same GNSS receivers in RESAJ and TLALOCNet that measure the locking on the ground also record the ionospheric TEC overhead — one instrument, two research uses. So if a credible seismo-ionospheric signal ever does get nailed down, this coast is already instrumented for it.

Tsunami · minutes, not days

If the ground shakes hard, don't wait for an alarm

A local tsunami from the offshore faults could reach the bay in minutes — faster than any official alert. The natural signs are the warning.

Natural warnings — act on any one
Strong or long shaking (hard to stand, ~20+ seconds) · sudden sea withdrawal exposing the seabed · a loud ocean roar. Any one = move to high ground now. Don't film, don't wait for sirens, don't drive if you can walk uphill faster.
  1. Inland and uphill — aim for ~20 m (60 ft) elevation or 1 km inland, whichever comes first. In Vallarta that's away from the Malecón and river mouths, toward the hills (Conchas Chinas, Amapas, eastern colonias).
  2. On foot if possible — roads jam instantly and high ground is close.
  3. Stay up for hours — the first wave is rarely the largest.
  4. Know two routes from home, work and the beach.
  5. Then follow Protección Civil Jalisco for the all-clear.

Ground response · geology meets the quake

Same quake, different shaking

The bridge between the seismic and flood stories: the soft river sediment that floods is also the ground that shakes hardest and can liquefy.

Downtown, the Malecón and the hotel zone sit on young alluvial / deltaic sediment from the Cuale and Ameca — loose, water-saturated. The hills (Conchas Chinas, Amapas, the Sierra edge) sit on bedrock. As seismic waves cross from rock into soft soil they slow and amplify, so the same earthquake shakes the flats harder than the hills — the effect that made distant quakes so destructive in Mexico City.

Conceptual cross-section (bedrock hills vs soft delta) — not a site-specific geotechnical map. A real Vs30 / soil-class study is needed per lot.
Two linked risks on the soft ground
Amplification — softer soil shakes harder, longer. Liquefaction — saturated sandy delta soil can briefly act like liquid in strong shaking, undermining foundations near rivers and the waterfront. Both peak exactly where the flood map is reddest.

Practical read: a beachfront slab on river-mouth fill has very different seismic exposure than a home on rock above Conchas Chinas — worth knowing before you buy, build, or retrofit.

The deltas — and where Banderas wins

The dry-season cluster. Nearly every Pacific and Caribbean resort shares the same prime window — roughly December through April: dry, warm, low humidity. That's when Vallarta, Cancún, Cozumel, Acapulco and Manzanillo are all at their best simultaneously, which is exactly why prices and crowds peak then.

Sargassum is the Caribbean's tax, and Vallarta doesn't pay it. This is the single biggest differentiator. Cancún, Tulum and (less so) Cozumel face sargassum seaweed roughly May–August, and 2026 is forecast to be a record year with arrivals as early as January. Vallarta, Cabo and the whole Pacific coast are sargassum-free — a decisive edge for a summer beach trip.

Heat & humidity split the Pacific. Acapulco and Manzanillo run hot and sticky almost year-round; Vallarta is meaningfully milder in winter (highs ~28°C vs ~31°C) with a real cool-down at night. Mérida is the heat outlier — April–May routinely hit 36°C+, making it a winter-only call. Cabo is the dry one: desert-low humidity, but its winter sea is cooler and rougher for swimming than Banderas Bay's calm water.

Hurricane exposure differs in kind. The Caribbean (Cancún/Tulum) and open Pacific points (Cabo, Acapulco) take direct hits — Acapulco's Otis (Cat 5, Oct 2023) and Cabo's Odile (2014) are the cautionary tales. Vallarta sits deep in a bay ringed by the Sierra Madre, which blunts most direct strikes, though it still gets heavy rain and surf June–November. Veracruz adds a quirk none of the others have: winter "nortes" — cold fronts that bring blustery, wet spells right in the high season.

The control. Mexico City proves the point by contrast — no beach, no hurricane, no sargassum, just mild highs (22–27°C) all year with afternoon storms May–October. It's the steady baseline the beach towns swing around. Tijuana is the cool Mediterranean outlier: its best months are the opposite of everyone else's (July–October), when the rest of Mexico is hot and wet.

Vallarta's verdict
Banderas Bay's edge isn't being the warmest or driest — it's the balance: a long Nov–Apr ideal stretch, calm swimmable water, mountain hurricane-shielding, no sargassum ever, and a summer where the rain politely confines itself to afternoons. Its only real weak window is Aug–Sep (peak heat, humidity and storm risk) — the same months you'd skip almost everywhere on this list except cool Tijuana and the high-altitude capital.

The read — and where Jalisco's highlands land

The Lake Chapala belt wins, and it's not folklore. Chapala and Ajijic top the table because the lake moderates an already-mild highland climate into something remarkably flat — warm dry winters, no real heat, no freeze. It's the climatic reason that strip of Jalisco became one of the densest expat retirement zones in the Americas. Guadalajara, just up the road, scores nearly as well with slightly more summer-storm and big-city edge.

The temperate-highland cluster is tight. Puebla, Oaxaca, San Miguel de Allende and the Mexico City control all land within a few points — mild dry winters, pleasant springs, and a forgiving summer rainy season of short afternoon storms. These are the "eternal spring" cities; their only knock is altitude rain June–September and chilly nights.

The north swings hard. Monterrey, Chihuahua and Ciudad Juárez are the volatility plays — genuinely brutal summer heat (Juárez tops 36°C, Monterrey hot and humid) stacked against winters that freeze and occasionally snow in the desert. Their best windows invert to spring and fall, threading between the two extremes. Toluca is the opposite problem: at 2,660 m it's the coldest major city, so chilly nights drag it down despite mild days.

The outlier. San Cristóbal de las Casas is cool and beautiful but sits in a wet highland — its May–October is genuinely soggy, which is what sinks its score. And Mérida, carried over from the beach list, is the reminder that "inland" isn't automatically mild: lowland Yucatán bakes April–May.

Beach vs inland, in one line
The coast trades hurricane and sargassum risk for warm winter swimming; the highlands trade the ocean for thin, dry, temperate air and a tidy summer rain habit. Vallarta (118) and the Chapala belt (132) bracket the question nicely — if you want water, the bay wins; if you want the most consistently comfortable air in Mexico, the lake does.

Field Guide No. 06 · Convective & Moisture Regime

Reading the Moisture Machine over Banderas Bay

A working meteorologist's lookbook for the Bahía de Banderas (CP 483xx). Not a forecast — a climatological diagnostic: when in the year, when in the day, and which mechanism to blame, plus the operational thresholds that separate a quiet afternoon from an arroyo flood. Built for someone who already reads a sounding; the prose stays out of your way.

Why this tab exists

PV doesn't get gentle, all-day stratiform rain. It gets a loaded boundary layer, a morning cap, and a terrain-anchored trigger — then it dumps. The hazard isn't the climatological mean; it's the variance. One tropical cyclone or one well-timed wave can deliver a month of rain between 16:00 and 22:00. This page maps the rhythms so the anomalies stand out.

01The annual rhythm

Köppen Aw, sharply seasonal. ~90% of annual rainfall falls Jun–Oct. Onset is early here — mainland-Jalisco coast fires roughly 2–4 weeks ahead of the US Southwest monsoon. September is the wet-season apex, where a mature monsoon overlaps the East Pacific TC peak.

02The daily rhythm

Two clocks run at once. Over land, the sea-breeze + orographic engine peaks late afternoon to mid-evening. Over the open bay, the land-breeze/longwave-destabilization mechanism peaks overnight into morning. Knowing which clock you're under tells you where to look first.

Morning cap → afternoon vent

Overnight subsidence + a clear morning are not a dry signal — they're how CAPE accumulates over a hot, saturated boundary layer. The cap holds until the sea-breeze front and terrain force breach it. Suppression in the morning often means a more violent evening, not a calmer one.

The foothill cumulus line

First nowcast tell: GOES visible/Cu field organizing along the Sierra El Cuale / Sierra de Vallarta crest 13:00–15:00. A continuous line = orographic mode (terrain-locked, slow movers). Scattered cells off a sea-breeze front = more mobile. Watch for back-building over the same arroyo.

03The mesoscale engine

A west–east schematic of the daytime convective machine: warm bay → coastal plain → the wall of the Sierra Madre Occidental rising immediately behind town. This is the geometry that turns ordinary tropical moisture into terrain-anchored heavy rain.

04Driver-by-driver — what to blame, by month

Six mechanisms, ranked by monthly contribution. Most heavy events are a stack: a moist monsoon boundary layer + a synoptic enhancer (wave, trough, or TC) landing on the diurnal trigger. Isolate the enhancer and you've found the anomaly.

05Operational clue checklist — nowcasting the dump

The signals a forecaster stacks through the day. None is sufficient alone; three or more aligned by midday is a heavy-rain afternoon. Thresholds are bay-column tuned, not generic CONUS values.

Stack these by ~12:00 LT

  • PWAT ≥ 50 mmColumn loaded. ≥60 mm = extreme / training potential. Pull from the 12Z Guadalajara/Manzanillo profiles or GFS/ECMWF column; the bay runs moister than either land station.
  • Ridge axis E of PV500 mb monsoon anticyclone centered over the plateau puts you in easterly steering on its south flank — the wave conveyor. Ridge too far west = drier, more subsident.
  • θe advection 850Low-level warm/moist advection off the warm pool. Rising surface Td through the morning with sea-breeze onset (~11:00–13:00, backing to W/SW) is the surface proxy.
  • SST ≥ 29 °COffshore fuel. Banderas Bay SST in the high-29s/30s (Jul–Oct, more so in El Niño) raises both the diurnal ceiling and TC-remnant potential.
  • Cu line on crestInitiation tell. GOES visible: cumulus organizing along the Sierra El Cuale 13:00–15:00. Continuous line → terrain-locked slow movers (worst flash case).
  • GLM lightning jumpIntensification tell. A rapid flash-rate increase precedes the heavy-rain core by ~10–20 min. Pair with any nearby radar (Manzanillo/Guadalajara coverage is marginal here — lean on satellite + lightning).
  • Vort-max in easterliesThe enhancer. A wave / inverted trough / 700 mb vorticity max riding the easterlies onto the coast is what turns a diurnal pulse into an organized, long-duration event.
  • TC / remnant within 500 kmHigh-end overprint. Even an offshore or weakening system streams deep moisture onshore — PV floods from systems that never make landfall. Sep is peak overlap.

06This season — 2026 standing watch

Live context · Jun 2026

NOAA now carries an El Niño Advisory: conditions are present and expected to strengthen into the 2026–27 winter (CPC, Jun 2026). Record-warm eastern-Pacific SSTs front-loaded the season — Amanda, Boris and Cristina were already named by the second week of June. Mexico's SMN forecasts 18–21 named EPAC systems; AccuWeather flags an above-average risk of direct impacts for Jalisco, Colima and Baja Sur. ENSO cuts both ways here — El Niño tends to suppress the interior diurnal monsoon (drier baseline highland convection) while enhancing tropical-cyclone intensity and rapid intensification and nudging tracks toward the mainland. Net for the Banderas coast: a quieter garden-variety afternoon regime punctuated by sharper, TC-moisture-driven heavy events. Watch the variance, not the mean — and §07 for the full picture.

07El Niño & Puerto Vallarta — what it usually means

Since the tab is standing watch on a live El Niño, here's the honest climatology: what an El Niño summer has historically done to this coast over past events — and, just as important, what it doesn't guarantee.

What El Niño does to the bay

Warm eastern-Pacific SSTs leave the water off Banderas hotter than normal (high-29s to low-30s °C), loading the column with moisture. More decisively, El Niño weakens the vertical wind shear over the East Pacific. Less shear plus more ocean heat is rocket fuel for tropical cyclones — not necessarily more storms, but a higher share of intense, rapidly-intensifying ones, with tracks statistically nudged toward mainland Mexico and Baja.

The paradox for routine rain

The same pattern tends to suppress the interior North American Monsoon, so the garden-variety highland afternoon convection runs drier. An El Niño summer in PV often feels quieter day-to-day — then delivers a season's rain in a handful of sharp, TC-moisture-driven dumps. The seasonal mean drops while the variance and the high-end tail climb. That's the hazard signature of this whole tab, turned up a notch.

El Niño years in living memory on this coast

A non-exhaustive look at how past El Niño seasons played on the Jalisco/Nayarit coast. The pattern isn't "more rain everywhere" — it's fewer but harder-hitting tropical systems, several of which rapidly intensified near the coast.

The honest caveat

El Niño raises the odds of an intense season and a mainland-favoring track — it does not guarantee a Vallarta hit. Most EPAC storms still recurve harmlessly offshore, and El Niño's strong upper-level winds sometimes shear systems apart right before landfall — Patricia collapsed from 215 mph to Cat 4 in hours before coming ashore. And not every PV flood needs an El Niño at all: Hurricane Nora drowned the Río Cuale in 2021, a La Niña year. The operational takeaway is exposure and readiness — a loaded basin means more chances — not a seasonal forecast you can plan a calendar around.

No corridor armed — log the check-in above.
Why the zones sit where they sit. The sierra sheds; the flats receive. The Ameca floodplain (Ixtapa–Las Juntas–Pitillal strip) collects the whole valley corridor. The Cuale and Pitillal drop ~1,000 m in under 15 km, so an "over the bay" night is an arroyo night for Centro and Romántica — the pin sits between their two outlets. Bucerías and Sayulita flood at their crossings; the south-shore coves take short, violent debris-fan pulses; the headlands just drain.
